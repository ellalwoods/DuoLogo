## Lesson A · What "original" means

### Card 1 · Rule
**Margin:** `s 32 CA`

**Original doesn't mean new, clever or good.**

Copyright subsists in an *original* literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work (s 32). In copyright, "original" sets a low bar. It doesn't mean the work is novel (that's patents), creative, beautiful or worthwhile. A shopping list, an exam paper or a train timetable can all be original.

It means two things:
1. The work **originates with its author**: it wasn't copied.
2. It is the product of the author's own **independent intellectual effort**.

**Go deeper:** The classic statement is *University of London Press v University Tutorial Press* (1916, UK): exam papers set by examiners were original literary works. The word "original", Peterson J said, is not concerned with originality of ideas or inventive thought, but with the expression of thought, and requires only that the work not be copied and originate from the author. Australian courts have always accepted that starting point. The arguments are all about point 2: *what kind* of effort counts?

---

### Card 2 · Rule
**Margin:** `s 32 CA` · `s 10 CA "literary work"`

**Why the question matters most for factual works.**

For a novel, a song or a painting, originality is rarely in doubt. The hard cases are **factual and functional works**: directories, databases, timetables, TV guides, forms, tables of data, computer-generated output.

These works take enormous *effort* to produce, but the effort mostly goes into **gathering facts**, and facts themselves are never protected. So the real question is whether the effort that went into the work is the kind copyright rewards.

**Go deeper:** s 10 includes "a table, or compilation, expressed in words, figures or symbols" in the definition of literary work. So compilations clearly *can* be protected. The question is always whether the particular compilation is *original*, and whether what the defendant took was the original part.

---

### Card 3 · Case
**Margin:** `(1917) HCA` · `(2002) FCAFC`

**The old Australian view: "sweat of the brow"**

For most of the 20th century, Australian law rewarded **skill *or* labour**. Hard work alone could make a compilation original.

- *Sands & McDougall v Robinson* (1917, HCA): a map compiled from other maps was original because of the work in compiling it.
- ***Desktop Marketing v Telstra*** (2002, FCAFC): Telstra's White and Yellow Pages directories were original because of the huge labour and expense of collecting and verifying the listings, even though their arrangement (alphabetical, by heading) involved no creativity.

**Go deeper:** In *Desktop*, the Full Court expressly declined to follow the US Supreme Court's decision in *Feist v Rural Telephone* (1991), which had rejected sweat of the brow and required a "modicum of creativity" in selection or arrangement. Australia, the Court said, protected industrious collection. That made Australia an outlier, and it gave owners of databases something close to a right in the information itself, which is what the High Court went on to question.

---

### Card 4 · Case
**Margin:** `[2009] HCA 14`

***IceTV v Nine Network*** (2009, High Court)

**Facts:** Nine published weekly schedules of its programs. IceTV built an electronic program guide. It made its own predictions of Nine's schedule, but checked and corrected them against published guides, which meant taking Nine's **time and title** information: which show, on which channel, at what time.

**Issue:** Did IceTV reproduce a **substantial part** of Nine's schedules, which were compilations?

**Held:** No (unanimously, in two joint judgments). The time and title information was factual. The skill and labour Nine had put into deciding *what to broadcast and when* was not effort directed to the *expression* of the schedule. The way that information was expressed was obvious and minimal, so IceTV hadn't taken a substantial part of what made the work original.

**Go deeper:** Formally *IceTV* is an infringement case (substantial part, unit 1.11). But because a "substantial part" is judged by the originality of what was taken, the Court had to say what originality means. Both judgments focused on **authorship** and on **effort directed to the particular form of expression**, and both expressed doubt about the reasoning in *Desktop*. Programming decisions are business decisions. Copyright isn't the right tool for protecting them.

---

### Card 5 · Case
**Margin:** `[2010] FCAFC 149`

***Telstra v Phone Directories*** (2010, Full Federal Court)

**Facts:** Telstra sued a rival directory publisher for copying its White and Yellow Pages: the same directories that had been protected in *Desktop* eight years earlier.

**Held:** **No copyright subsisted.** The directories were produced largely by automated computer systems. Many people contributed along the way, but no identifiable **human authors** had exercised independent intellectual effort directed to the material form of the directories. Without human authors, there is no original work.

**Why it matters:** After *IceTV* and *Phone Directories*, the sweat-of-the-brow approach in *Desktop* is effectively no longer good law. **Originality is tied to authorship**: you must be able to identify the human author(s) and show that *their* effort produced the expression.

**Go deeper:** Gordon J at first instance and the Full Court (Keane CJ, Perram and Yates JJ) both treated authorship and originality as two sides of one question. The Full Court noted that the effort of the people who collected and checked the data was directed to the *information*, not to its expression. Many commentators see the result as aligning Australia with *Feist*, though Australian courts haven't adopted a "creativity" test as such.

---

### Card 6 · Test
**Margin:** `s 32 CA` · `IceTV` · `Phone Directories`

**The modern originality test, step by step**

Use this structure in an exam answer:

1. **Identify the work** and its type (literary, dramatic, musical, artistic; a compilation?).
2. **Identify the author(s)**: human beings whose effort produced it.
3. **Not copied:** did the work originate with them?
4. **Independent intellectual effort:** did they exercise skill, judgement or effort of their own?
5. **Directed to expression:** was that effort directed to the *material form* of the work, rather than only to gathering facts or making business decisions?

If all five are met, the work is original. For compilations, the originality often lies in the **selection or arrangement**.

---

### Card 7 · Trap
**Margin:** `EXAM`

**Three classic mistakes**

1. **Saying "original" means "novel" or "creative".** It doesn't. Say "originates with the author, independent intellectual effort directed to expression".
2. **Citing *Desktop Marketing* as current law** without flagging that *IceTV* and *Phone Directories* have overtaken its sweat-of-the-brow reasoning.
3. **Forgetting authorship.** Originality and authorship go together after *Phone Directories*. If you can't name a human author, you probably don't have an original work.

### Lesson A questions (8)

1. **Multiple choice.** A university lecturer writes a 20-question exam paper using standard textbook questions she rephrased herself. Is it original?
   - ✅ *Yes: it originates with her and reflects her own effort in selecting and expressing the questions.* (Quality and novelty are irrelevant: *University of London Press*.)
2. **True / false.** Following *Desktop Marketing*, the labour of collecting facts is enough to make a compilation original. → **False** today; doubted in *IceTV*, effectively superseded by *Phone Directories*.
3. **Case Snap.** Match each case to its role: *Sands & McDougall* → old skill-or-labour approach · *Desktop* → sweat of the brow, rejects *Feist* · *IceTV* → effort must be directed to expression · *Phone Directories* → no human author, no original work.
4. **Order.** Put the five steps of the originality test in order.
5. **Multiple choice.** In *IceTV*, why didn't Nine's programming decisions count? ✅ *They were business decisions about what to broadcast, not effort directed to expressing the schedule.*
6. **Spot the error.** "Telstra's directories are protected because Telstra spent millions verifying listings (*Desktop*)." → The error: relying on *Desktop*; after *Phone Directories*, no copyright subsisted in those directories.
7. **Multiple choice.** Which is the best one-line definition of originality? ✅ *The work originates with a human author and results from their independent intellectual effort directed to its expression.*
8. **From an earlier unit (1.4).** Are the facts in Nine's schedule protected by copyright? → **No.** Facts are never protected; only their expression can be.

## Lesson B · Hard cases

### Card 8 · Rule
**Margin:** `s 10 CA "literary work"`

**Compilations: where the originality hides**

In a compilation, the facts are free. The originality, if any, is in:
- **Selection:** choosing what goes in and what's left out, using judgement.
- **Arrangement:** organising it in a way that reflects choices.
- **Presentation:** the written expression around the facts (reviews, descriptions, commentary).

Anyone may take the facts. Taking the selection, arrangement or presentation may infringe.

**Go deeper:** This is why the same information can sit in two compilations, one protected and one not. It's also why an infringer who rebuilds the facts independently, in their own arrangement, usually doesn't infringe, even if the underlying data ends up identical.

---

### Card 9 · Worked example
**Margin:** `APPLY`

**Two Brisbane café lists**

*The Brunch List* is a guidebook naming Brisbane's 50 best brunch spots. Its editor visited 300 cafés, picked 50, ranked them, and wrote a short review of each.

*CaféData* is a spreadsheet of every licensed café in Brisbane, exported automatically from council registration records and sorted by postcode.

- *The Brunch List* is **original**: identifiable human author, with independent judgement in the selection, ranking and reviews, all directed to expression.
- *CaféData* is **probably not**: no human selection, an automatic and obvious arrangement, and effort (if any) went into collecting facts. Like *Phone Directories*, there's no human author of the expression.

If a rival publishes its own top 50 using some of the same cafés, it takes facts, not *The Brunch List*'s selection, so it's unlikely to infringe. If it copies the ranking and reviews, it probably does.

---

### Card 10 · Case
**Margin:** `[2012] FCAFC 16`

***Acohs v Ucorp*** (2012, Full Federal Court)

**Facts:** Acohs's software generated material safety data sheets. When a user entered data, the system automatically produced the HTML **source code** for each sheet. Acohs claimed copyright in that source code.

**Held:** No copyright in the source code. It was generated by the computer program, not written by a human. The programmers wrote the *program*, and the data-entry staff entered *information*, but no human author produced the source code as a work.

**Why it matters:** Confirms *Phone Directories*: **computer-generated material without a human author is not an original work.** This is the starting point for the AI question (card 11).

---

### Card 11 · Rule
**Margin:** `Phone Directories` · `Acohs` · ⚑

**What about AI-generated work?**

No Australian court has yet ruled directly on generative AI output ⚑. But the existing cases point clearly:

- **Purely AI-generated output** (a prompt, then accept what comes out) probably has **no human author**, so no copyright.
- **Human contribution directed to expression** may be enough: substantially editing, rewriting, selecting and arranging AI outputs into a larger work. The copyright then covers the human contribution.
- A **short prompt** is unlikely to be enough on its own. A long, detailed prompt that dictates the expression is an open question.

**Go deeper:** Compare the US Copyright Office's approach (human authorship required; AI-generated portions disclaimed) and the UK's s 9(3) CDPA, which gives copyright in "computer-generated works" to the person making the arrangements. Australia has no equivalent of s 9(3). Whether it should is a live policy debate ⚑.

---

### Card 12 · Case
**Margin:** `[1989] PC` · `[2010] FCA`

**Too small, or just copied?**

***Interlego v Tyco*** (1989, Privy Council): LEGO redrew its old engineering drawings of bricks with small technical changes, hoping to restart the copyright term. Held: **not original**. Skill and labour in *copying* doesn't create a new original work, unless something visually significant is added.

***Fairfax Media v Reed International*** (2010, Federal Court): newspaper **headlines** were generally too short to be literary works in their own right, like titles and slogans. (The articles and the newspaper as a whole were protected.)

**Go deeper:** Translations, arrangements of music, and edited editions *can* be original works, because they involve fresh judgement about expression. The line is between reproducing and re-expressing. *Exxon v Exxon Insurance* (UK, 1982) is the classic authority that a single invented word isn't a literary work: it conveys no information or pleasure on its own. Brand names are a job for trade marks.

### Card 13 · Compare
**Margin:** `COMPARE`

**Australia vs elsewhere**

| | Australia | US | UK / EU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test | Independent intellectual effort directed to expression (*IceTV*, *Phone Directories*) | Independent creation + a modicum of creativity (*Feist*) | Author's own intellectual creation (EU, via *Infopaq*) |
| Sweat of the brow? | Rejected in practice since 2009–10 | Rejected (1991) | Rejected |
| Database right? | **None** | None | Yes: EU *sui generis* database right (and UK version) |
| Computer-generated works | No human author → no copyright | Not protected | UK: s 9(3) protects them |

---

### Card 14 · Critique
**Margin:** `ESSAY`

**Should Australia protect databases?**

**For:** Databases cost a fortune to build and are easy to copy. Without protection, there's less incentive to invest (the incentive theory, unit 0.1). The EU gives a separate database right for this reason.

**Against:** The information itself should stay free. A low originality bar creates monopolies over facts (*IceTV*'s concern). The EU's own review (2005 and later) found little evidence that the database right increased database production. Contract, technical protection and confidentiality already protect investment.

**The current position:** Australia has no database right, and the post-*IceTV* originality test leaves many factual compilations unprotected. A good essay weighs both and uses *Desktop* → *IceTV* → *Phone Directories* as the story of the shift.

---

### Card 15 · Trap
**Margin:** `EXAM`

**In a problem question about a compilation, always separate:**
1. **Subsistence:** is the compilation original at all? (Authors? Effort directed to expression?)
2. **Infringement:** did the defendant take a **substantial part** of *what made it original* (the selection, arrangement or expression), or just the facts? (Unit 1.11, *IceTV*.)

Students often merge these. Examiners notice.
