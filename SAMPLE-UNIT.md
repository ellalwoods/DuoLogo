## Lesson A · What "original" means

### Card 1 · Rule
**Margin:** `s 32 CA`

**Original doesn't mean new, clever or good.**

Copyright subsists in an *original* literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work (s 32). In copyright, "original" sets a low bar. It doesn't mean the work is novel (that's patents), creative, beautiful or worthwhile. A shopping list, an exam paper or a train timetable can all be original.

It means two things:
1. The work **originates with its author**: it wasn't copied.
2. It is the product of the author's own **independent intellectual effort**.

**Go deeper:** The classic statement is *University of London Press v University Tutorial Press* [1916] 2 Ch 601: exam papers set by examiners were original literary works. Peterson J said that copyright is "not concerned with the originality of ideas, but with the expression of thought", and that the Act requires only "that the work must not be copied from another work — that it should originate from the author" (at 608–609). In *IceTV* the High Court put the Australian requirement the same way: the work must originate "with an author or joint authors from some independent intellectual effort" (French CJ, Crennan and Kiefel JJ at [48]). The arguments are all about point 2: *what kind* of effort counts?

**Source:** CA s 32(1)–(2) (text checked). WJ, "Originality" (*ULP* facts, holding and quotation; *IceTV* [48]).

### Card 2 · Rule
**Margin:** `s 32 CA` · `s 10 CA "literary work"`

**Why the question matters most for factual works.**

For a novel, a song or a painting, originality is rarely in doubt. The hard cases are **factual and functional works**: directories, databases, timetables, TV guides, forms, tables of data, computer-generated output.

These works take enormous *effort* to produce, but the effort mostly goes into **gathering facts**, and facts themselves are never protected. So the real question is whether the effort that went into the work is the kind copyright rewards.

**Go deeper:** s 10(1) says "literary work" includes "(a) a table, or compilation, expressed in words, figures or symbols; and (b) a computer program or compilation of computer programs". So compilations clearly *can* be protected. The question is always whether the particular compilation is *original*, and whether what the defendant took was the original part.

**Source:** CA s 10(1), definition of "literary work" (quoted verbatim). WJ, "Compilations and Databases".

### Card 3 · Case
**Margin:** `(1917) 23 CLR 49` ⚑ · `[2002] FCAFC 112`

**The old Australian view: "sweat of the brow"**

For most of the 20th century, Australian law rewarded **skill *or* labour**. Hard work alone could make a compilation original.

- *Sands & McDougall v Robinson* (1917, HCA): a map compiled from other maps was original because of the work in compiling it. ⚑
- ***Desktop Marketing v Telstra*** [2002] FCAFC 112: Telstra's White and Yellow Pages directories were original because of the substantial labour and expense that went into them, even though a "whole of universe" listing like the White Pages involved no selection, and its arrangement (alphabetical, by heading) involved no creativity. Much of the data simply came in from subscribers: Telstra's effort went mainly into systems for **verifying and storing** the listings, and that was enough.

**Go deeper:** The Full Court held that a compilation is original if it results *either* from skill, judgement or knowledge in selecting, presenting or arranging the material, *or* from substantial labour or expense in collecting it. It did not follow the US Supreme Court's decision in *Feist v Rural Telephone* (1991), which had rejected sweat of the brow and required "some minimal degree of creativity". Australia, the Court said, protected industrious collection. The Court also noted that Desktop could have gathered all the same facts by its own inquiries without infringing. That made Australia an outlier, and it gave owners of databases something close to a right in the information itself, which is what the High Court went on to question.

**Source:** WJ, *Desktop Marketing* and *Feist* boxes (holding, the two routes to originality, verification and storage, the independent-inquiry point; Lindgren J at 472–478 and Sackville J at 539 of the report). *Sands & McDougall* is not covered by WJ and the judgment couldn't be opened: ⚑ facts and citation unconfirmed.

### Card 4 · Case
**Margin:** `[2009] HCA 14` · `(2009) 239 CLR 458`

***IceTV v Nine Network*** (2009, High Court)

**Facts:** Nine prepared a Weekly Schedule of its programs, which it supplied to aggregators who published TV guides. IceTV built an electronic program guide, the IceGuide. In doing so it took **time and title** information (which show, on which channel, at what time) from the aggregators' published guides.

**Issue:** Did IceTV reproduce a **substantial part** of Nine's Weekly Schedule, a compilation?

**Held:** No. All six judges agreed, in two joint judgments (French CJ, Crennan and Kiefel JJ; Gummow, Hayne and Heydon JJ). The time and title information was factual, and its expression "is not a form of expression which requires particular mental effort or exertion": the authors had "little, if any, choice" in how to express it. A chronological arrangement was "obvious and prosaic". So IceTV hadn't taken a substantial part of what made the Schedule original.

**Go deeper:** IceTV had **conceded** at trial that the Weekly Schedule was an original literary work, so the High Court's remarks on originality for subsistence are strictly obiter. But because a "substantial part" is judged by the originality of what was taken, the Court still had to say what originality means. French CJ, Crennan and Kiefel JJ warned that rewarding skill and labour "without any real consideration of the productive effort directed to coming up with a particular form of expression" can lead to error (at [52]). Gummow, Hayne and Heydon JJ said the reasoning in *Desktop* "may be ... out of line with the understanding of copyright law over many years", and its emphasis on "labour and expense" per se must be treated "with some caution". Programming decisions are business decisions. Copyright isn't the right tool for protecting them.

**Source:** WJ, *IceTV* box (facts; the concession; quotations from [42]–[43], [48], [52], [152] and [157]–[160], and the closing remarks on *Desktop*). The number of judges and the two joint judgments come from WJ's attributions; the judgment itself couldn't be opened.

### Card 5 · Case
**Margin:** `[2010] FCAFC 149`

***Telstra v Phone Directories*** (2010, Full Federal Court)

**Facts:** Telstra again claimed copyright in its White and Yellow Pages: the same directories that had been protected in *Desktop* eight years earlier.

**Held:** **No copyright subsisted.** The directories were produced largely by automated computer systems applying a set of "Rules". Many people contributed along the way, but Telstra could not identify the **human authors**, and the people involved had not exercised independent intellectual effort directed to the form of the directories. Without human authors, there is no original work. The Full Court dismissed Telstra's appeal, and the High Court refused special leave.

**Why it matters:** *Desktop* has not been formally overruled, but after *IceTV* and *Phone Directories* its sweat-of-the-brow reasoning must be treated with caution and is no longer a safe guide. **Originality is tied to authorship**: you must be able to identify the human author(s) and show that *their* effort produced the expression.

**Go deeper:** Gordon J at first instance put it this way: "Authorship and originality are correlatives ... Start with the work. Find its authors. They must have done something, howsoever defined, that can be considered original" (at [344]). On appeal Keane CJ said *IceTV* "authoritatively establish[es]" that the focus is not on preventing misappropriation of skill and labour "but upon the protection of copyright in literary works which originate from individuals" (at [96]), and that the effort needed "is not required to rise to the level of 'creativity' or 'inventiveness'" (at [58]). Effort spent building and checking the database was directed to the *information*, not to its expression. Many commentators see the result as aligning Australia with *Feist*, though Australian courts haven't adopted a "creativity" test as such.

**Source:** WJ, *Telstra v Phone Directories* box (Gordon J's two findings and [344]; Keane CJ at [58] and [96]; appeal dismissed; special leave refused). ⚑ Not confirmed from the judgment: the other Full Court judges (Perram and Yates JJ) and the medium-neutral citation of the first-instance decision.

### Card 6 · Test
**Margin:** `s 32 CA` · `IceTV` · `Phone Directories`

**The modern originality test, step by step**

Use this structure in an exam answer:

1. **Identify the work** and its type (literary, dramatic, musical, artistic; a compilation?).
2. **Identify the author(s)**: human beings whose effort produced it.
3. **Not copied:** did the work originate with them?
4. **Independent intellectual effort:** did they exercise skill, judgement or effort of their own?
5. **Directed to expression:** was that effort directed to the *particular form of expression* of the work, rather than only to gathering facts or making business decisions?

If all five are met, the work is original. For compilations, the originality often lies in the **selection or arrangement**.

**Source:** Built from CA s 32 and the passages cited in cards 4–5 (WJ: *IceTV* [48], [52]; *Phone Directories*, Gordon J at [344], Keane CJ at [58], [96]). The five-step order is our teaching structure, not a court's.

### Card 7 · Trap
**Margin:** `EXAM`

**Three classic mistakes**

1. **Saying "original" means "novel" or "creative".** It doesn't. Say "originates with the author, independent intellectual effort directed to expression".
2. **Citing *Desktop Marketing* as current law** without flagging that *IceTV* cast doubt on it and *Phone Directories* reached the opposite result on the same directories. (It has not been formally overruled: say "treated with caution".)
3. **Forgetting authorship.** Originality and authorship go together after *Phone Directories*. If you can't name a human author, you probably don't have an original work.

**Source:** WJ ("While Desktop Marketing has not been explicitly overruled, it must now be treated with caution"; Gordon J at [344]).

### Lesson A questions (8)

1. **Multiple choice.** A university lecturer writes a 20-question exam paper using standard textbook questions she rephrased herself. Is it original?
   - ✅ *Yes: it originates with her and reflects her own effort in selecting and expressing the questions.* (Quality and novelty are irrelevant: *University of London Press*.)
2. **True / false.** Following *Desktop Marketing*, the labour of collecting facts is enough to make a compilation original. → **False** as a statement of current law: doubted in *IceTV*, and *Phone Directories* found no copyright in the same directories. (*Desktop* has not been formally overruled.)
3. **Case Snap.** Match each case to its role: *Sands & McDougall* → old skill-or-labour approach · *Desktop* → sweat of the brow, declines to follow *Feist* · *IceTV* → effort must be directed to expression · *Phone Directories* → no human author, no original work.
4. **Order.** Put the five steps of the originality test in order.
5. **Multiple choice.** In *IceTV*, why didn't Nine's programming decisions count? ✅ *They were business decisions about what to broadcast, not effort directed to expressing the schedule.*
6. **Spot the error.** "Telstra's directories are protected because Telstra spent millions verifying listings (*Desktop*)." → The error: relying on *Desktop*; in *Phone Directories* the Full Court held no copyright subsisted in those directories.
7. **Multiple choice.** Which is the best one-line definition of originality? ✅ *The work originates with a human author and results from their independent intellectual effort directed to its expression.*
8. **From an earlier unit (1.4).** Are the facts in Nine's schedule protected by copyright? → **No.** Facts aren't protected; only their expression can be.

## Lesson B · Hard cases

### Card 8 · Rule
**Margin:** `s 10 CA "literary work"`

**Compilations: where the originality hides**

In a compilation, the facts are free. The originality, if any, is in:
- **Selection:** choosing what goes in and what's left out, using judgement.
- **Arrangement:** organising it in a way that reflects choices.
- **Presentation:** the written expression around the facts (reviews, descriptions, commentary).

Anyone may take the facts. Taking the selection, arrangement or presentation may infringe.

**Go deeper:** This is why the same information can sit in two compilations, one protected and one not. It's also why an infringer who rebuilds the facts independently, in their own arrangement, usually doesn't infringe, even if the underlying data ends up identical (the point the Full Court made even in *Desktop*). In *IceTV*, Gummow, Hayne and Heydon JJ located the Weekly Schedule's originality "not in the provision of time and title information, but in the selection and presentation of that information together with additional programme information and synopses" (at [152]).

**Source:** CA s 10(1). WJ, "Compilations and Databases" (independent intellectual effort "in selecting, presenting or arranging the information"); *Desktop* box; *IceTV* [152].

### Card 9 · Worked example
**Margin:** `APPLY`

**Two Brisbane café lists**

*The Brunch List* is a guidebook naming Brisbane's 50 best brunch spots. Its editor visited 300 cafés, picked 50, ranked them, and wrote a short review of each.

*CaféData* is a spreadsheet of every licensed café in Brisbane, exported automatically from council registration records and sorted by postcode.

- *The Brunch List* is **original**: identifiable human author, with independent judgement in the selection, ranking and reviews, all directed to expression.
- *CaféData* is **probably not**: no human selection, an automatic and obvious arrangement, and effort (if any) went into collecting facts. Like *Phone Directories*, there's no human author of the expression.

If a rival publishes its own top 50 using some of the same cafés, it takes facts, not *The Brunch List*'s selection, so it's unlikely to infringe. If it copies the ranking and reviews, it probably does.

**Source:** Fictional facts. Applies cards 4–8.

### Card 10 · Case
**Margin:** `[2012] FCAFC 16` ⚑

***Acohs v Ucorp*** (2012, Full Federal Court) ⚑

**Facts:** Acohs's software generated material safety data sheets. When a user entered data, the system automatically produced the HTML **source code** for each sheet. Acohs claimed copyright in that source code.

**Held:** No copyright in the source code. It was generated by the computer program, not written by a human. The programmers wrote the *program*, and the data-entry staff entered *information*, but no human author produced the source code as a work.

**Why it matters:** Follows *Phone Directories*: **computer-generated material without a human author is not an original work.** This is the starting point for the AI question (card 11).

**Source:** ⚑ **Unverified.** *Acohs* is not discussed in WJ, and the judgment (AustLII, Federal Court) couldn't be opened from this environment. Facts, holding and citation are as drafted; check against the judgment before release.

### Card 11 · Rule
**Margin:** `Phone Directories` · `Acohs` · ⚑

**What about AI-generated work?**

No reported Australian decision has yet ruled directly on copyright in generative-AI output (as far as could be checked, November 2026 ⚑). But the existing cases point clearly:

- **Purely AI-generated output** (a prompt, then accept what comes out) probably has **no human author**, so no copyright.
- **Human contribution directed to expression** may be enough: substantially editing, rewriting, selecting and arranging AI outputs into a larger work. The copyright then covers the human contribution.
- A **short prompt** is unlikely to be enough on its own. A long, detailed prompt that dictates the expression is an open question.

**Go deeper:** Compare the US Copyright Office's approach (human authorship required; AI-generated portions disclaimed) and the UK's s 9(3) CDPA, which gives copyright in "computer-generated works" to the person by whom the arrangements necessary for their creation are undertaken ⚑. Australia has no equivalent provision: nothing in the current Act deems a person the author of computer-generated material. On the input side, the federal government said in October 2025 that it would not introduce a text-and-data-mining exception for AI training ⚑.

**Source:** CA (Compilation No. 65): no computer-generated-works provision; the *Copyright Amendment Act 2026* dealt with orphan works, remote learning and other measures, not AI. ⚑ Not checked against primary sources: the absence of any Australian AI-output decision (a search of secondary sources found none; the court databases couldn't be opened); UK s 9(3) CDPA (legislation.gov.uk not reachable); US Copyright Office guidance; the October 2025 TDM announcement (press reports only).

### Card 12 · Case
**Margin:** `[1989] AC 217` ⚑ · `[2010] FCA 984`

**Too small, or just copied?**

***Interlego v Tyco*** (Privy Council) ⚑: LEGO redrew its old engineering drawings of bricks with small technical changes, hoping to restart the copyright term. Held: **not original**. Skill and labour in *copying* doesn't create a new original work, unless something visually significant is added.

***Fairfax Media v Reed International*** [2010] FCA 984: newspaper **headlines** were generally too short to be literary works in their own right. Bennett J: headlines, "like titles, [are] simply too insubstantial and too short to qualify for copyright protection as literary works" (at [44]), though evidence about a particular headline might be enough (at [46]). Authorship couldn't be assumed either: "A headline that does no more than repeat a phrase from the article is not an original literary work" (at [84]). (The articles and the newspaper as a whole were protected.)

**Go deeper:** Translations, arrangements of music, and edited editions *can* be original works, because they involve fresh judgement about expression. The line is between reproducing and re-expressing. *Exxon Corp v Exxon Insurance Consultants* [1982] Ch 119 is the classic authority that a single invented word isn't a literary work: although "Exxon" was original, it wasn't substantial enough for copyright to subsist in it. Brand names are a job for trade marks.

**Source:** WJ, *Fairfax v Reed* box ([44], [46], [84]); *Exxon* box. ⚑ *Interlego v Tyco* is not in WJ and the judgment couldn't be opened: facts, holding and year unconfirmed. (The report is [1989] AC 217; the advice may have been delivered in 1988, which matters because the syllabus uses judgment years. Check.)

### Card 13 · Compare
**Margin:** `COMPARE`

**Australia vs elsewhere**

| | Australia | US | UK / EU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test | Independent intellectual effort directed to expression (*IceTV*, *Phone Directories*) | Independent creation + "some minimal degree of creativity" (*Feist*) | EU: the "author's own intellectual creation" (*Infopaq*, C‑5/08, 2009) |
| Sweat of the brow? | Doubted since 2009–10; *Desktop* not formally overruled | Rejected (1991) | Rejected |
| Database right? | **None** | None | EU *sui generis* right for a "substantial investment" in obtaining, verifying or presenting contents (Directive 96/9/EC, art 7); UK has its own version ⚑ |
| Computer-generated works | No human author → no copyright | Not protected ⚑ | UK: s 9(3) CDPA protects them ⚑ |

**Source:** WJ (*Feist*, *Desktop*, *IceTV*, *Phone Directories*). EUR-Lex: *Infopaq* judgment of 16 July 2009 ("original in the sense that it is its author's own intellectual creation"); Directive 96/9/EC art 7(1) (quoted). ⚑ UK and US entries not checked against primary sources.

### Card 14 · Critique
**Margin:** `ESSAY`

**Should Australia protect databases?**

**For:** Databases cost a fortune to build and are easy to copy. Without protection, there's less incentive to invest (the incentive theory, unit 0.1). The EU gives a separate database right for this reason.

**Against:** The information itself should stay free. A low originality bar creates monopolies over facts (*IceTV*'s concern about "anti-competitive behaviour"). The European Commission's first evaluation of the Database Directive (December 2005) found no proven effect of the *sui generis* right on database production ⚑. Contract, technical protection and confidentiality already protect investment.

**The current position:** Australia has no database right, and the post-*IceTV* originality test leaves many factual compilations unprotected. A good essay weighs both and uses *Desktop* → *IceTV* → *Phone Directories* as the story of the shift.

**Source:** WJ, *IceTV* [52]. EUR-Lex, Directive 96/9/EC. ⚑ The 2005 evaluation (DG Internal Market and Services working paper, 12 December 2005) is summarised from secondary sources; its PDF has moved off ec.europa.eu and couldn't be opened.

### Card 15 · Trap
**Margin:** `EXAM`

**In a problem question about a compilation, always separate:**
1. **Subsistence:** is the compilation original at all? (Authors? Effort directed to expression?)
2. **Infringement:** did the defendant take a **substantial part** of *what made it original* (the selection, arrangement or expression), or just the facts? (Unit 1.11, *IceTV*.)

Students often merge these. Examiners notice.

**Source:** WJ, *IceTV* box ("Whether a selection or arrangement of elements constitutes a substantial part of a work depends on the degree of originality of that selection or arrangement", [42]–[43]).
